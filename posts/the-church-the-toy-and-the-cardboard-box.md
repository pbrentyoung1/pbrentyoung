The most surprising thing at my church this year is who keeps showing up.

For the past couple of years, I’ve been serving at an Anglican parish, all organ and robed choir and centuries-old liturgy, about as traditional as a church gets. Yet our pews have been filling with people from the far opposite end of the spectrum, folks raised on bands and lights, who by every reasonable measure should find our services strange, even cold. If what held people to a church were its style and its content, they would have visited once and never come back. Instead they’ve stayed. It’s made me question something most of us take for granted about why anyone loves the church they call home.

You can watch the same thing unfold, more slowly, in any family new to town. Somewhere between unpacking the kitchen and finding the good grocery store, they begin the quiet search that so many families begin: where will we belong on Sunday?

Over the next month or so they'll visit four or five churches. They'll hear thoughtful preaching, sing with capable musicians, shake hands with kind people, and sit under congregations that plainly love Jesus. And somewhere along the way, one of those churches will begin to feel different from the rest. Not better on paper, but somehow more like home. They'll find themselves drawn back, sensing they could belong there, quietly moved to take a next step. If you could ask them what drew them, they might struggle to name it. They couldn’t point to a single thing on a website or schedule. It was simply how it felt to be there.

## The overlap is bigger than we think

Stand back far enough and most churches look remarkably alike. We preach the Bible. We sing songs. We run a children's ministry and a student ministry. We gather people into small groups. We serve our neighbors, support missionaries, stream the service, and keep a fresh pot of coffee going in the lobby. If you drew a Venn diagram of everything the churches in your town offer, the overlap in the middle would be enormous, and the little slivers that make each church distinct would be far smaller than most of us like to imagine.

None of that is a criticism. These are good and faithful things, and a family looking for a church fully expects to find them. That’s exactly the point. When something is expected, it usually isn’t what draws a person in. From where a visitor sits in the back row, most of what we’re proudest of is simply what it means to be a church at all. It often isn’t what makes a family sense they’ve found home.

## When everything overlaps, experience becomes the difference

So what is it that draws a family in? Not the list of ministries, which looks about the same everywhere you go. What differs from one church to the next isn’t what you offer but what it *feels like to encounter it.* When nearly everything else cancels out, experience becomes the difference between the circles.

Think about what a visitor is most likely to carry home.
- Whether anyone noticed they were standing alone in the lobby.
- How hard it was to find the nursery, and how they felt the moment they handed over their child.
- Whether the greeter's smile seemed genuine or rehearsed.
- Whether people from the congregation welcomed them.
- How long it took for someone to follow up, if anyone did at all.
- Whether they felt seen as a person before they were counted as a volunteer slot to fill.

These are the things people remember, and they’re nearly impossible for another church to copy, because they aren’t a program. They are the fruit of a hundred small intentions quietly working together.[^1]

> ! People rarely remember the ministries you offered. They remember how it felt to be a part of your community.

This is a humbling thought for those of us who pour our best hours and budgets into content. We labor over the sermon, and we should. But the sermon is usually not what a first-time guest remembers a week later. They remember the experience that surrounded it.

Every parent has felt a smaller version of this. You spend a small fortune on the gift, wrestle it free of its packaging, and ten minutes later your child has climbed inside the cardboard box. It’s tempting to take it personally, but the people who study how children play say the child isn’t being ungrateful. The expensive toy came with a script (Push the button, hear the song, watch the thing happen). *It asked the child to sit and receive. The box came with no script at all, and that was the gift.* It left room for the child to imagine, to build, to belong to whatever the box became. **The toy made the child an audience. The box invited the child to help create, and we remember most what we had a hand in making.**

> "A toy that performs can place the child in the role of audience rather than creator."
>
> [Laura Dimler, PhD](https://lauradimler.substack.com/p/why-your-child-loves-the-box-more)

We can drift toward the toy without ever meaning to, refining a service so polished and complete that it quietly asks the newcomer to do nothing but watch. But people aren’t looking for a performance to admire from the back row. They’re looking for room to belong. The content is the gift, and it genuinely matters. Yet the thing a family carries home is so often the box, the space we made for them to be part of something rather than spectators to it. *Experience outlasts content in the memory, and it does more than we think to draw a family back and help them believe they could belong.*

## Why they stayed

I told you I couldn’t fully explain it. The families themselves have done better. When I ask them why they’ve stayed, not one of them mentions the music or the liturgy or even the sermons. They talk about being welcomed. They talk about being known. They talk about finding a community that made room for them when they were bruised and unsure. And here’s the part that still moves me. In a setting utterly unlike anything they were used to, a lively world of bands and open hands traded for kneelers and creeds, many of them are meeting Christ in ways they never had before. The content couldn’t have been more different. It was the experience of being loved and belonging that carried them home.

> ! People may cross a canyon of worship style on a bridge of relationship.

## Where the experience begins

Here’s what makes this even weightier. That experience doesn’t begin in the lobby. It begins long before anyone shakes a hand or hears a note of music. It begins on a search results page at ten o'clock at night, on a website loading over morning coffee, in a Google listing and a scroll through your social feed, on a sign at a confusing intersection, in the tone of a confirmation email, in a registration form that either works on a phone or doesn’t, in whether the nursery information was easy to find or buried three clicks deep, in the way a volunteer answers the question that took someone a week to work up the courage to ask. Long before a family meets your worship team or your preaching or your children's ministry, they’ve already met your church through its communication. You didn’t make your first impression in the parking lot. You made it days earlier, on a screen, and you may not have realized you were making it.

This is why communication was never a department that simply promotes the ministry. It’s ministry, the first and often the quietest form of welcome. Every unanswered message, every dead link, every form that breaks, every sign that points the wrong way is a small barrier standing between a person and the next step you’re praying they’ll take. To clear those barriers is to make room, the same room the box left for the child, the same room a community made for those families who came to us from so different a world. Frontline ministries create moments. Communication creates expectations. When the welcome people expect matches the welcome they find, trust grows. When it doesn’t, trust quietly erodes, often before anyone has said a word to them at all.

## The thing worth remembering

We can’t make our churches less alike by piling on more, and we probably shouldn’t try. The overlap is a good thing. It means the Church is holding a great deal in common. But it does mean that the experience people have, beginning with the very first one, deserves the same care and prayer we give the words we say from the stage. A family will forget most of what we taught them on a given Sunday. They won’t soon forget how it felt to be welcomed by us. That’s worth tending, because it’s so often the difference between a visit and a home.

## Make room for someone this week

Choose a church you’ve never visited and plan to attend one Sunday. Begin with the first search result. Open the website on your phone. Find the service times, the address, and the children's information. Look for what to expect and what your next step should be. If there’s a registration or contact form, notice whether it makes the path clearer or adds another question.

Then go. Pay attention to the communication that guides you from the street to your seat, and notice whether the experience agrees with what the church led you to expect. Afterward, return to your own church's communication with those questions still fresh. What do you help people understand? What do you assume they already know? Where might they feel uncertain? Choose one unnecessary barrier your team can remove this week.
