Churches are often thrilled to receive the Google Ad Grant. Then the account sits dormant.

It may contain a few campaigns advertising Sunday worship, a long list of keywords someone guessed during setup, and a homepage receiving clicks it was never designed to serve. The church technically has access to as much as $10,000 per month in search advertising, but very little of that opportunity is doing meaningful work.[^1]

The problem usually isn't a lack of good intentions. It is a lack of clarity about who the church is trying to reach, what those people are already searching for, and what the church can offer when they arrive. It is a lack of landing pages, articles, booklets, videos, courses, checklists, and other resources that answer the questions behind the search. It is also a lack of the technical expertise and weekly attention required to keep the account healthy.

> ! The grant doesn't create a ministry strategy. It amplifies the one you have.

That's why these aren't twenty clever ads to run. They are twenty ways your church can connect a question to a useful answer, then make one appropriate next step visible.

## Before you build twenty campaigns

An Ad Grant account won't become useful simply because your team adds more keywords. Before choosing an idea from this list, your church needs to answer a few larger questions.

- Who are you trying to reach?
- What are they carrying?
- What language do they use when they search?
- What can your church speak about with honesty, care, and real experience?
- What resource will answer the question after the click?
- What next step can you offer without rushing the relationship?

You also need someone who knows how to translate those answers into a working search account. Google requires specific account structure, geographic targeting, relevant keywords, meaningful conversion tracking, and ongoing performance management.[^2] A pastor, communications director, or volunteer may help shape the ministry behind the campaign, but most churches will need a trained partner to build and tend the technical system.

The companion essay [Your Church's Google Ad Grant Is a Garden](/blog/your-churchs-google-ad-grant-is-a-garden) explains why this work can't be treated as a one-time setup. For now, begin with this pattern:

**Search → Answer → Trust → Next Step → Relationship**

## Find the questions first

The twenty ideas below are places to begin listening, not twenty assumptions about what your community needs.

Use tools such as [KeywordsPeopleUse](https://keywordspeopleuse.com/), Google's Keyword Planner, and Bing Webmaster Tools Keyword Research to discover the words and questions people are actually using.[^4] Study your Google Search Console data and the search terms already appearing in your Ad Grant account. Add what your pastors, ministry leaders, receptionists, and volunteers hear from real people every week.

Then look for the meeting place between a real question, a specific audience, and something your church is prepared to answer with substance.

> ! Answer questions people are asking. Don't answer questions and then look for people to ask them.

Don't build a campaign because your church happens to have an old booklet sitting in a folder. Confirm that people are asking the question, then decide whether the booklet, landing page, video, course, or checklist actually answers it. Search research chooses the question. Your church's knowledge and ministry experience shape the answer.

## 1. Answer basic questions about Christian faith

People search questions such as “Who is Jesus?”, “What is grace?”, “How do I become a Christian?”, and “What do Christians believe?” Your church can build a clear library of articles and short videos that answer those questions without assuming church experience or theological vocabulary.

The next step might be another question, a beginner's course, a conversation with a pastor, or a simple invitation to learn more. The first job is to give the person the answer you promised.

## 2. Explain your church's tradition

Someone may be searching “What do Methodists believe?”, “What is Anglican worship?”, or “Baptist beliefs about baptism?” A thoughtful belief guide or short course can explain your tradition in plain language, including what someone might experience in worship.

This is more useful than sending the person to an “About Us” page written for people who already understand your denominational language.

## 3. Help someone learn to pray

“How do I pray?” is not a request for an advertisement. It is a request for help.

Build a simple page that answers the question, then support it with a short pastoral video, a downloadable prayer guide, or a seven-day email series. The campaign becomes useful because there is something useful behind it.

## 4. Help someone begin reading the Bible

A person searching for a beginner's Bible reading plan may not know where to start, which translation to choose, or what to do when the text becomes difficult. Your church can create a reading plan, a printable guide, a video introduction, or a short course that makes the first step less intimidating.

An invitation to a class or study can follow, but it shouldn't replace the answer they came to receive.

## 5. Welcome people returning to church

Some people aren't searching for church for the first time. They are searching for a way back after years away, a painful experience, a divorce, a season of doubt, or a move that disrupted their spiritual life.

Honestly help them find a church, even if it isn’t your church. The goal is to love people, not sell them on your particular church.

A page titled “Thinking About Coming Back to Church?” can acknowledge the hesitation, answer common questions, and offer a gentle next step without pretending that returning is easy.

## 6. Prepare someone for a first visit

“Church near me” is only the beginning of the decision. People also want to know what to wear, where to park, where their children will go, how long the service lasts, whether they will be singled out, and what worship will feel like.

A first-visit page, photographs, a brief walkthrough video, accessibility information, and clear service details can turn a generic search ad into real hospitality.

## 7. Offer grief support

Grief often sends people searching at hours when no church office is open. Your church can create a resource that names what grief feels like, explains the support available, answers questions about the group or counselor, and gives the person a clear way to reach someone.

The page should help even if the reader never registers. A booklet, video, prayer, or list of trustworthy local resources can make the page an act of care rather than a thin event advertisement.

## 8. Support marriages and families

People search for help with communication, conflict, parenting, becoming a new parent, caring for aging parents, and keeping a family connected through change. A church can build specific guides, conversation checklists, classes, videos, and support paths around those moments.

Avoid promises that a course will fix a marriage or remove a family's pain. Offer what your church can honestly provide and make professional referrals clear when they are needed.

## 9. Connect people with recovery and care ministries

Someone may search for addiction recovery, divorce care, trauma support, or a group for caregivers before they ever search for a church. A ministry-specific landing page should explain who the group serves, what happens at a meeting, who leads it, what privacy someone can expect, and how to ask a question before attending.

The ministry can become a front door, but only if the page is written for the person standing outside it.

## 10. Make local assistance easier to find

Food pantries, clothing closets, financial coaching, English classes, job support, and other community ministries are often buried several layers inside a church website. Search campaigns can connect people directly to the help they need.

The resource behind the ad must stay current. Hours, eligibility, required documents, location, language access, and contact information are part of the ministry, not administrative details to update later.

## 11. Meet people at life transitions

Moving, marriage, divorce, a new baby, retirement, caregiving, and loss create questions that don't fit neatly inside a Sunday-service campaign. Your church can build content around the transition itself, then connect people to resources, groups, or conversations that help.

People usually search for the threshold before they search for the organization that can help them cross it.

## 12. Serve the Christian calendar

Advent, Christmas, Lent, Holy Week, and Easter create recurring search interest. Do more than advertise an event. Answer “What is Advent?”, offer a devotional, explain Ash Wednesday, provide a Holy Week guide, or help a parent talk with a child about Easter.

Seasonal campaigns need seasonal tending. Plant them early enough to grow, update every date and detail, and prune them when the season ends.

## 13. Invite people into introductory courses

Christianity courses, membership classes, Bible introductions, and theological seminars can answer searches from people ready for more than an article but not yet ready for a large commitment.

The landing page should explain who the course is for, what questions it explores, how much prior knowledge is expected, how long it lasts, and what participation feels like. “Register now” is not enough information to help someone decide whether the room was prepared for them.

## 14. Help people find community

Searches for Bible studies, men's groups, women's groups, young-adult gatherings, senior activities, and groups for parents reveal a desire for connection. A campaign can lead to a group finder, but the resource needs more than names and meeting times.

Show what the gathering is like, who someone will meet, whether the group is open, and how to ask a question. Belonging begins before attendance when communication removes the fear of arriving alone.

## 15. Create resources for children, students, and parents

Parents search for youth activities, children's programs, Bible resources, camps, preschool information, and support for difficult conversations at home. Your church can create age-specific guides, parent checklists, program videos, safety information, and clear introductions to the leaders who will care for their children.

The campaign should speak to the parent's real questions, not merely announce that the ministry exists.

## 16. Connect people with opportunities to serve

“Volunteer opportunities near me” may come from someone who has never attended your church. Search campaigns can introduce food distribution, mentoring, foster-care support, disaster response, neighborhood projects, and other work where the church welcomes community participation.

Describe the difference the work makes, what the volunteer will actually do, what training is required, and the smallest honest first step.

## 17. Recruit specialized volunteers and leaders

Some ministries need musicians, production volunteers, counselors, translators, photographers, teachers, group leaders, board members, or people with professional expertise. Broad volunteer language may never reach them.

A focused page can name the role, the responsibility, the preparation required, and the mission it serves. Specificity helps the right person recognize that their experience can be invested here.

## 18. Extend sermons, podcasts, and videos

Do not advertise a podcast merely because your church produces one. Advertise the question a specific episode answers.

Someone is more likely to search “How does trauma affect faith?” than the title of your show. Send them directly to the episode, include a written summary or transcript, add related resources, and offer a next step that continues the subject rather than promoting the whole content archive.

## 19. Share downloadable tools

Prayer booklets, Advent devotionals, Bible reading plans, marriage conversation guides, volunteer checklists, and ministry toolkits can give a searcher something useful to keep. A download can also begin an email relationship when the exchange is clear and the follow-up honors what the person requested.

Do not make the resource sound free if the actual price is surrendering an email address to unrelated promotion. Tell people what they will receive and what communication will follow.

## 20. Use search as a listening tool

One of the best uses of the Ad Grant may be learning what your future congregation is actually asking. Search terms can reveal language and needs that your staff would never have guessed.

Bring those questions back to your ministry and communications teams. They can shape sermons, articles, videos, classes, website navigation, support resources, and future campaigns. The account becomes more than a distribution channel. It becomes one way your church listens to the community it has been called to serve.

## Twenty ideas still need one plan

This list can become another pile if your church tries to build everything at once.

Choose one audience, one question, and one resource your church is genuinely prepared to create. Build the entire path from search to answer to next step. Measure something meaningful, such as a resource download, course registration, support request, volunteer inquiry, or conversation. Google requires active and accurate conversion tracking, but good stewardship asks whether the action represents real help rather than an inflated number.[^3]

Then tend it. Read the search terms. Remove what doesn't belong. Improve the ad and the answer together. Listen to what people do next. Let what you learn shape the next resource you plant.

The opportunity is not simply to spend free advertising money. It is to notice the questions people are already carrying and prepare something worth finding.

Which question is your church ready to answer first?
