Every pastor has the same recurring nightmare, and like any good horror movie, it opens with dead calm.

The church is full and still. You take a breath, ready to preach. Somewhere in the dark, though, something has already gone horribly wrong.

Then you hear it. A crackle. A low hum that was not there a second ago. The volunteer at the sound booth freezing, then lunging in slow motion for a fader. And then, like a jump scare in a cheesy movie: a squeal of feedback that snaps every head in the room toward you. Or worse, the slow, creeping silence of a microphone that has quietly died, a congregation of people realizing it at the same moment you do.

Cold sweat runs down the middle of your back. Can they hear me? Do I switch mics? Is the battery going? Do I keep talking, or do I stop?

You are alone at the front of a room full of people, and the monster is not a masked figure in the wings. It is the tech.

Here is the cruelest twist of all. In that moment, the thing that was supposed to carry your message has **become** the message. No one in the room, including you, is thinking about what you are about to say. Everyone is only thinking about whether the tech is going to work. And for the rest of the sermon, so are you.

This is why churches approve sound budgets, and why they invest in the people who run them. Sound isn’t the message. The message deserves a faithful carrier.

Good sound is clear, balanced, reliable, and quiet in all the right ways. When the system and the engineer are doing their jobs well, people in the room aren’t thinking about microphones or gain structure or cables. They are just listening to the message.

> ! It takes a lot of experience to know how to get out of the way.

Getting out of the way is not the same as being absent. It means doing the work so well that it disappears. The sound is present. The engineer is busy. The system is doing an enormous amount. All of it is serving something bigger than itself. Your communications should work exactly the same way.

## A faithful carrier

For many pastors, the language of marketing sits wrong. It sounds commercial. It feels manipulative, or at least a world away from discipleship. Part of what I am trying to do across these pieces is give you a reference for what communications actually is, reframed in the language of your church. And the clearest place to start is next to something your church already trusts: audio.

Communications doesn’t have to create the message to matter. Just like a sound system, its only job is to carry the message well, so that nothing gets between what you are saying and the person it is for.

There is a line about design that says it perfectly:

> "Design should never say, 'Look at me.' It should always say, 'Look at this.'"
>
> — [David Craib](http://www.paragraphic.ca/where-it-all-began/)

Great sound says, "Listen to this." Great communications says, "Pay attention to this." The craft is deliberate, but it is never about itself. Its whole job is to help people meet the message instead of tripping over the medium.

## Growth changes the responsibility

A faithful carrier gets out of the way. But how much work that takes is not fixed. It scales with the room.

When twenty people gather in a living room, almost any sound setup works. One mic, one speaker, one volunteer who often remembers where the power button is. Done. But the setup that serves twenty in a living room will not serve two hundred in a sanctuary, and it will not come close to two thousand in an auditorium.

As a church grows, the work gets more specialized. The gear has to get more capable. The people running it need more training, planning, workflows and artistry. What used to take a single willing volunteer eventually requires a team of professionals. It’s not because the mission changed. It’s because the responsibility grew.

Upgrading the system is not extravagance. Most of the time it is just faithful stewardship. Reaching five hundred people with clear, dependable sound is simply harder than reaching fifty in a living room.

Communications is no different. The static website, the email list, the one social account, and the "just add it to the announcements" habit that served a small congregation buckles under a bigger, busier one. More people means more needs, more front doors, and more ways for what’s important to get lost.

The gospel does not need an upgrade. The care we give its carriers will.

## The message doesn't change. The mix does.

Growth does not only call for a bigger sound system. It often calls for more mixes.

An advanced church might run a front-of-house mix for the room, an in-ear mix for the platform, a livestream mix for the people watching at home, and a recording mix for whoever presses play on Tuesday. One message. Many mixes. Each one shaped for the people on the other end.

Communications works the same way. The same message travels through your website, your email, your social feeds, your podcast, your newsletter. Each has its own audience, its own rhythm, its own expectations. Pasting the same words into all of them is not clarity. Faithful communication asks who is on the other end of each channel, and shapes the message so they can actually receive it.

The message does not change. The mix does.

## Sound and communications at a glance

| Principle | Sound | Communications | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clarity | Every word is intelligible | Every message is understood | People act on what they grasp, not on what they miss |
| Noise reduction | No hiss, hum, or feedback | No clutter, jargon, or mixed signals | Distraction always competes with the message |
| Transparency | You forget the system is there | You forget the medium and meet the message | The craft should point past itself |
| Balance | Voice, music, and room in proportion | The right weight on the one thing that matters | If everything is emphasized, nothing is |
| Artistry | Mixed with taste, not just accuracy | Told with beauty, not just correctness | Beauty earns attention and honors the subject |
| Reliability | It works this Sunday, and the next | It works every time someone shows up | Trust is built by consistency |
| Reach | Fills the room and the livestream | Meets people on the channels they already use | The message is for the person in the back row too |
| Channels and mixes | Front of house, monitors, stream, recording | Website, email, social, podcast, print | Each audience receives it differently |
| Technology and systems | Capable, well-maintained gear | Working tools and clear workflows | Good people still need good instruments |
| Skill and experience | A trained engineer, not just a volunteer | Trained communicators, not just willing ones | Craft takes practice, not only good intentions |
| Growth and complexity | A bigger room needs a bigger system | A bigger church needs bigger communications capacity | The mission holds; the responsibility grows |

## The quiet work of getting out of the way

Communications is discipleship work, because it is part of how people find, understand, and respond to the unique story of your church. A clean website does not make a disciple. A timely email does not change a life. A clear next step does not create the gospel. But bad communications builds walls. Confusing language, a stale website, the announcement nobody saw, the form that never worked, the message sent down the wrong channel: any one of them can keep a person from hearing, or understanding, or taking the next step.

The sound engineer did not write the sermon or the song. But the mix is how the room hears it. Your communications team does the same job the other six days of the week, carrying the message clearly and honestly and appropriately down every channel it travels.

If the message deserves a faithful carrier on Sunday, doesn't it deserve one on Monday too?

That is the work. Not to get the system noticed, but to serve the message so well that people can give it their whole attention. So the pastor can stop watching the sound booth, and just preach.
