There is a moment that happens in churches all over the country. Someone gets the email saying the church has been approved for the Google Ad Grant, reads the words "up to $10,000 per month," and gets very excited.[^1]

Then they log in.

What should we advertise? Which words should we use? Where should each ad send people? What should we measure? Why aren't we spending the whole grant? Who is going to look at this again next week? Why does my ad keep failing approval?

The excitement of receiving the grant runs headfirst into the responsibility of creating and managing it. Your church was given access to an extraordinary communications tool, but wasn't given a strategy, a library of helpful content, meaningful landing pages, working conversion tracking, or the time and experience required to manage any of it.

That's where many Google Ad Grant accounts begin to go quiet. The church has the grant. It simply has no idea what to do with it so it just sits there month after month.

## Free media is not free expertise

Google Ad Grants gives qualifying nonprofits access to as much as $10,000 each month in search advertising. That's real value, but it isn't a check and it isn't a guarantee that the full amount will be spent. It's access to media inside a system that still has to be planned, built, measured, and improved.

When I first went through Google's certification process, the product was still called AdWords. The training took about six hours. The current path I recently reviewed had grown to seven courses totaling more than twenty-two hours. That doesn't include the time it takes to gain the practical experience Google itself recommends before certification.[^2]

The platform has become too complex for the average (or even above average) church staff member to manage as one more task squeezed between the bulletin, Sunday slides, website, social posts, email, and whatever emergency arrived this morning. That isn't an indictment of the staff member. Google Ads is now a deeply specialized skill.

The account needs useful campaigns, tightly related keywords, relevant ads, specific landing pages, accurate conversion tracking, appropriate bidding, geographic targeting, and regular attention to performance and policy. Google requires Ad Grant accounts to maintain specific structures and meaningful measurement. Most accounts must maintain a 5 percent monthly click-through rate, and many must record at least one valid conversion each month.[^3] This isn't a tool you hand to the person who happens to have an open hour on Thursday.

**This is one of those areas where your church needs a partner.**

That partner might be a trained person on staff, a qualified volunteer, a consultant, or an agency. The title matters less than the relationship. Someone needs enough experience to work with your church, understand your mission, translate that mission into a search strategy, and keep tending the account after the first campaigns are live.

## A garden, not a building

Churches often approach an Ad Grant like a building project. Set it up. Finish it. Hand someone the keys. Walk away.

But a Google Ad Grant account isn't a building. It's a garden.

You have to plan what belongs there and prepare the ground. You have to plant according to the season. Then you have to weed, feed, prune, fertilize, and pay attention to what is actually growing. Some ideas will flourish. Others will never take root. A campaign that was healthy six months ago may now be crowded by irrelevant searches, weakened by a stale landing page, or pointed toward a ministry that no longer meets.

> ! Google gives your church the ground. It doesn't grow the garden for you.

## What is faithful to sponsor?

Some churches make it through the complexity and still hesitate at the word "Sponsored." The label can feel crass, commercial, and strangely unspiritual. We are comfortable paying to print a postcard, place a sign beside the road, or broadcast a service. But when a search result admits that we paid for its placement, it can feel as if we have crossed a line.

The sponsored label isn't the problem. In fact, it's an honest label. It tells the person exactly why that result appeared where it did. The more important question is what your church has chosen to place behind it.

> ! Paying to distribute a message doesn't make the message unfaithful. What matters is whether the message faithfully serves the person who receives it.

A post is worth sponsoring when it begins with a question someone is already asking and offers a truthful, generous answer. A clear introduction to your church's beliefs may be worth sponsoring. So may a guide to prayer, an article about returning to church, a grief resource, an explanation of what happens in a worship service, information about a support group, or a thoughtful answer to a difficult question about faith.

These pieces don't have to hide who created them. They shouldn't imitate a neutral resource and reveal the church only after someone clicks. The church can speak plainly about who it is, why it created the resource, and what it believes. Honest sponsorship is an invitation, not a disguise.

The line is crossed when the content exploits fear, grief, loneliness, or spiritual uncertainty to force a response. That line is also crossed when the ad promises an outcome the church can't guarantee, when the landing page avoids the question that earned the click, or when every answer becomes a thin excuse to say, "Come to our church."

Before sponsoring a post, ask whether it would still be worth creating if the person who reads it never attends your church. If the answer is yes, you are probably beginning from a place of service.

## Begin with the person who is searching

The most common mistake is to begin with what the church wants.

We want more visitors. We want more volunteers. We want people to register for the event. We want to build the email list.

Those may be worthwhile outcomes, but none of them explains why someone opened Google. People rarely begin by searching for the thing your church wants from them. They search for the question, pain, or possibility already in front of them.

They search for "How do I pray?" or "What do Methodists believe?" They search for grief support nearby, a Bible study for beginners, help for a struggling marriage, or what happens at a church service. They may be returning to faith after years away. They may be curious about Christianity but not ready to walk through a church door.

The search isn't just a keyword. It's a person telling you what they need help with right now.

That changes the job of the ad. Instead of interrupting someone with what your church wants to announce, the ad can lead them toward a thoughtful answer. The landing page can actually answer the question. Then, after helping, it can offer one honest and appropriate next step.

**Search → Answer → Trust → Next Step → Relationship**

That's the real opportunity inside the Google Ad Grant. The ad isn't standing on a digital street corner shouting the name of your church. It's listening at the crossroads for someone already asking a question your church may be able to answer.

## Find the questions before you build the answers

Churches often begin content planning inside a staff meeting. We decide what we want to say, create an article or video about it, and then go looking for people who might care.

Search gives us the opportunity to reverse that order.

> ! Answer questions people are asking. Don't answer questions and then look for people to ask them.

Tools such as [KeywordsPeopleUse](https://keywordspeopleuse.com/) can gather questions from Google's People Also Ask results, autocomplete, and other places where people reveal their language. Google's Keyword Planner can suggest related phrases and show estimated search demand. Bing Webmaster Tools includes its own Keyword Research tool with related terms, question keywords, search volume, and trends.[^5]

Your existing data can help too. Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools can show the searches where your current pages already appear. Your Ad Grant's search terms report shows the actual language that triggered an ad. Those are not merely optimization reports. They are records of what people were trying to find.

Don't let a tool replace the people themselves. Listen to the questions pastors hear after worship. Notice what parents ask before they register a child. Read the emails sent by people considering a first visit. Ask ministry leaders which concerns surface again and again. Search research becomes most useful when it joins the lived knowledge already present in your church.

The goal is not to find the largest possible number. A question asked by twenty people in your community may matter more to your mission than a broad phrase searched thousands of times somewhere else. The goal is to find the honest intersection between what people are asking and what your church is prepared to answer well.

## The ad needs something behind it

Showing up beside a question is not the same as answering it.

Search ads need content and resources behind them. Landing pages, articles, booklets, videos, courses, checklists, email series, and ministry conversations all have to work hand in hand with the questions your campaigns are built around. The ad may earn the click, but the resource has to keep the promise.

If someone searches "How do I pray?" it isn't enough for your church to appear at the top of the page and say, "We can help." Where does the click go? Perhaps it leads to a page that answers the question simply, a short video that helps someone pray for the first time, a downloadable guide they can keep, and an invitation to receive a week of prayers by email. Each piece continues the same answer at a different depth.

If someone is looking for grief support, the ad shouldn't lead to a generic homepage and leave them to search again. It might lead to a page that acknowledges what they are carrying, explains the support your church offers, includes a brief pastoral video or booklet, answers the questions someone may be afraid to ask, and offers a clear way to contact a real person.

This is where the work becomes larger than advertising. The search campaign and the content plan have to grow together. Your communications team may identify the question, but pastors and ministry leaders help answer it. Designers and writers shape the resource. The website creates a welcoming path through it. Follow-up helps the relationship continue.

> ! You can't advertise an answer your church hasn't taken the time to create.

Sometimes the most important thing your Ad Grant reveals isn't a keyword you should buy. It's a resource your church still needs to make.

## Build entry points, not one large invitation

Many church accounts are built around one campaign and one invitation: come visit us Sunday.

But your future congregation isn't standing in one place asking one question. One person is curious about faith. Another needs help carrying grief. Someone else wants to understand your tradition. A family that has just moved into town needs to know whether their child will be welcomed. A longtime Christian is looking for a place to serve.

Your account can become an ecosystem of entry points:

| Campaign family | The question behind the search | The resource that answers | A faithful next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discover | What do Christians believe? | An introductory article or video | Keep exploring a question |
| Understand | What does this church or tradition believe? | A belief guide or short course | Experience it in worship or conversation |
| Help | Is there support for what I am facing? | A booklet, pastoral video, or support page | Contact someone or join a group |
| Visit | What would it be like to come? | A first-visit page, photographs, and walkthrough video | Plan a first visit |
| Connect | Is there a place for me here? | A group finder or ministry story | Attend a gathering |
| Grow | How can I go deeper? | A course, Bible reading plan, or devotional | Begin a study or discipleship path |
| Serve | Where can I make a difference? | A service guide or opportunity profile | Explore a specific opportunity |

These aren't labels for people or measures of spiritual maturity. They are ways to plan communication around the question someone is asking and the next step your church can honestly offer.

Notice what this requires. A good ad can't rescue a vague or unhelpful page. If someone searches "What do Methodists believe?" and the ad sends them to a homepage filled with service times and staff photographs, the church hasn't answered the question. If someone searches for grief support and lands on a calendar with no explanation of the group, the campaign has created another barrier.

The grant reveals the content your church needs to grow.

## Let the garden teach you

This may be the most valuable part of the whole program. The Ad Grant can become a listening tool.

Search data can show you the language people actually use, not the language your staff assumes they use. It can reveal questions about prayer, anxiety, forgiveness, marriage, denominations, worship, community, and faith. Those questions can inform far more than advertising. They can shape articles, podcasts, sermon resources, classes, frequently asked questions, email series, and the structure of your website.

An article answering "What does the Bible say about anxiety?" might begin as a landing page for a search campaign. It could lead to a seven-day Scripture guide, then to a pastoral resource or group. Over time, the article may begin to earn organic search traffic too.

The paid campaign helps you discover what to plant. Your content gives the searcher something worth finding. Your measurement shows whether the path led anywhere meaningful. Then what you learn shapes the next season.

This is why clicks alone are not enough. Google requires active and accurate conversion tracking, but stewardship asks a better question than compliance: did the person find a useful next step? A newsletter signup, resource download, volunteer form, support request, call, or registration can tell you far more than a homepage visit.[^4]

> ! Measurement should help you care for the garden. It should never make a person feel like produce being harvested from it.

## What a partner should help you cultivate

Your partner's job isn't simply to log in and keep the account from being deactivated. A faithful partner should help your church connect search intent to ministry strategy.

They should be asking what people in your community are searching for, what need that search reveals, what honest answer your church can offer, and what next step is appropriate. They should help you see where a campaign needs a new landing page rather than another variation of ad copy. They should bring search language back to your communications and ministry leaders so the account keeps learning from the people it hopes to serve.

And they should tend it with you. Every week.

That's different from buying a one-time setup. A setup can give you campaigns. A partnership can help those campaigns remain accurate, useful, compliant, and connected to the changing life of your church.

## Plant one complete path

If your church already has the grant but doesn't know what to do next, don't begin by trying to spend all $10,000. Begin with one real question.

What is one thing people in your community are already searching for that your church is genuinely prepared to help with?

Choose the question. Create the clearest answer you can. Offer one appropriate next step. Make sure that step can be measured. Then watch what happens and tend the path for a month.
